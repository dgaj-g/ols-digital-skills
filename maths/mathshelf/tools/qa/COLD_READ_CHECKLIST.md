# THE MATHSHELF COLD-READ CHECKLIST — v1, 5 September 2026

**This file is what the judge is handed, and nothing else.** It lives here, committed with the platform, so that what the judge was told is always inspectable. (The copy in `Claude Work/Maths/` was the design-time seed and is superseded by this one.)

The judge receives exactly two things: **the rendered transcript** — the pupil transcript of ONE book in the order she meets it, or the teacher transcript of the markbook in the order a teacher meets it — and **this file**. No design pack, no diff, no source, no content map, no authoring conversation. The judge's verdict file names the transcript's hash; the pack refuses a mismatch. That is DFM 270, and it is a rule about the reader's context, not about their diligence.

---

## 0. THE DEFAULT IS FAIL

**A sentence FAILS unless it reads clean aloud at the reader's age. "Not wrong" is not a pass.** Grammatical is not a defence. A sentence with no banned word, breaking no rule, can still be one a twelve-year-old stumbles over, and no mechanical net catches that class. Where you are unsure, the answer is FAIL. A sentence you read twice has failed. A sentence you would have to defend has failed (DFM 231). **Deleting is a legitimate fix** — an aside that needs a paragraph of explaining goes.

**The reader is an eleven or twelve-year-old, whatever the book's cover says** (DFM 256). The Angles and Algebra books are sat by J3 (13/14); the Handling Data books by S1 GCSE (15/16). Voice may be a notch more grown-up on the GCSE shelf; the sentences may not be harder. Read every book as the same reader.

---

## 1. THE READER, AND WHERE SHE IS SITTING

She is eleven or twelve. She may be at a school Chromebook or iPad with the class, or on her phone at home revising, so she may have a **mouse or a finger** — a sentence that says "tap" or "click" as if there were only one way in is untrue for half the room; the screen says what to DO ("choose", "press", "put", "move", "drag"), and names both when it must name a gesture. Her connection may be the school's or her own; "the wifi" is never what a sentence blames. **There may be no teacher in the room** — homework, revision, a cover lesson — so on that screen the screen is the teacher (DFM 53). She can replay the exercise's short film from every question, but she may not have watched it yet.

What is scarce for her is **reading age and confidence**, not time. Spend words freely; keep each sentence short. One idea per sentence; as many sentences as understanding needs.

What is on her screen, and can be named: the light **page** (the paper) with the question; the **dock** underneath it holding what she works with — a **number pad**, a **tray** of tiles or markers, a row of **chips** to choose from, a **nudge pad** (four arrows) — the **Check** button ("Mark my working", "Mark my answers", "Mark my comparison"); a **grid** with a **curve**, a **rule** she slides, **points**, a **scale** with **markers**; the **marks** drawn beside her work; the **tally**; the **film** strip; the **contents** strip; the **shelf** of books; her **book**'s gold **star**.

## 2. THE QUESTIONS, ON EVERY SENTENCE (DFM 24/138)

Read it **aloud**, cold, from her seat. Then answer:

1. **Can she DO it** where she is sitting, with what is on her screen — and does she know WHICH thing to press?
2. **Can she PICTURE every noun** in it — has she met that thing on a screen she has already seen in this transcript, or in an earlier book on the shelf?
3. **Can she SAY what it is for?**
4. **What would she ASK here?** Any question she would have to ask is a FAIL, because there may be nobody to ask (DFM 193d).
5. **Does it give the answer away?** A stem, a chip, a tray order, a hint or a piece of feedback that tells her where the point goes, which value is the median, or which chip is right, has failed — the film teaches the method; the question makes her bring it.

Any "no" to 1–3, or any "yes" to 4–5, is a FAIL.

## 4. THE SHAPE RULES — mechanical enough to be sure about

- **No fragment chains.** A verbless clause joined to another by a comma is banned in pupil prose (DFM 192b). Every sentence has a verb.
- **Sequences are numbered lists**, one number per line, left-aligned (DFM 135/171/274) — a dock hint with three steps is a list, not a sentence.
- **The action comes first** on a question: what she is asked to DO, then how, then what the finished thing should look like (DFM 151). The instruction about HOW to use the tray or pad sits ABOVE it, never under it.
- **Define before use, in shelf order.** A term is defined when the film of THIS book has said it in plain words before the question uses it, or an earlier book on the shelf has — and each book must stand alone, so its own film defines what it uses. "Cumulative frequency", "upper class boundary", "interquartile range", "quartile", "box plot", "whisker", "line of best fit", "correlation", "stem", "leaf", "key", "sector", "midpoint", "modal class", "hypothesis", "sample", "biased" — each has a first meeting, and it is in a film caption.
- **The metaphor travels with the real name** at first use on a surface (DFM 267g/268): "the running total (the cumulative frequency)", "the middle value (the median)".
- **The person leads the sentence.** "You…", never "The app will…".
- **Concrete nouns.** "the number pad", "the tray", "the scale", "the curve" — never "the interface", "the tool", "the system".
- **Device-neutral verbs.** Never a bare "tap" or "click" as THE gesture (§1).
- **True to the screen.** If it says five markers, there are five in the tray. If it says the curve, the curve is on her screen. If it says "your teacher can see this", the teacher can. Numbers appear only when they are real and matter.
- **Feedback fits what she did.** A tap kind is never praised for "working" or "lines"; a plot is praised for points and a curve; a judgement for weighing the evidence.
- **UK English.** Never "math", never US spellings.
- **No cleverness**: no idioms, no poetry, no personification, no pedagogy or design rationale on stage, no internal names (statcore, the rule as a convention, pencil/ink, the press), no dead product names, no taglines.

## 5. THE PER-ITEM BLOCK — every question, in writing (DFM 233/235, extended for maths)

Answer all ten, per question. A tick is not a judgement.

1. **Exactly one defensible answer** under the book's own conventions? If a careful reader could argue a second (a different valid route, the range where the IQR was meant, a half-square either side), the item is broken — or the conventions on screen are.
2. **Anchored scenario?** A real thing she can picture (heights of pupils, a wage, a javelin throw), from the source, never a floating "some data".
3. **Does the difficulty measure anything?** An item everyone gets right measures nothing.
4. **Do the options, the tray or the layout hand the answer over?** A pre-drawn target, a coloured tile, a first-slot answer, a tray in sorted order, a chip pressed by default, an odd-one-out by length.
5. **Can you say the item's point in one sentence?**
6. **Does the stem or any hint telegraph the method** (the boundary, the formula, "half the total")?
7. **Is every wrong route a real misconception with a named slip**, and is the slip's name in words she and her teacher would use?
8. **Is the marks split fair for what she actually does** — method marks on the steps she takes, the accuracy mark on the thing she reads or places?
9. **Does the feedback, right and wrong, fit this kind** (points and curve, not working and lines)?
10. **Does the wrong-answer state teach without giving away** — the true positions appear only after the second attempt, and the film is one press away?

Plus: **every pronoun in a scenario points backward at a name already given** (DFM 267d).

## 6. THE TEACHER TRANSCRIPT — the second register (DFM 138.3, 257)

The teacher is a maths colleague who has never opened this markbook, at a laptop or a smartboard, between two lessons. What is scarce for her is **time**. Economy is achieved by CUTTING sentences, never by compressing one into a fragment that costs meaning. Read every teacher sentence aloud and ask:

1. **Can she DO it from this screen** — which thing to press is named as it is printed?
2. **Does every number, glyph and colour say what it is** — the book, the exercise, the question it belongs to; a permanent key for ✓ ◐ ✗² ● — and for the flag chips?
3. **Is every platform word glossed at its first meeting** (Slips, Full grid, Needs you, Set-up, the book → exercise → question ladder, "tap a mark to change it")?
4. **Does a control that will not act say why, beside itself?**
5. **What would a non-specialist ASK here?** Any question is a defect. The ease law (feedback_ease_and_beauty_law): a markbook that needs explaining has failed.
6. **No she/her for a generic pupil** (the pupil, by name); no examiner jargon; no fragment flourishes; no rhetorical triples; no invented staff; UK English.

## 7. HOW TO FILE IT (DFM 270 — written for HIM to read)

The verdict file (`MATHS_COLD_READ_VERDICTS_<book>.md` or `MATHS_COLD_READ_VERDICTS_TEACHER.md`) opens by naming the transcript artefact and its **TRANSCRIPT HASH**, copied from the transcript's own header. The pack refuses a verdict whose hash is not the current transcript's.

Then **one row per sentence judged**, quoting the sentence, in plain English, so he can open the file and read what the judge said about any line before he sits the book. No trade jargon, no rule numbers standing in for a reason.

**Every READ-FIRST CANDIDATE named by the language gate has its own row, answered by its path.** The gate emits that list; the pack checks each one is answered.

**Every question has its per-item block** (§5), filed under the question's path.

A row reads:
```
| VERDICT | where it is (book › section › question › field) | the sentence, quoted | what a reader would trip over, or why it is clean |
```
**PASS**, **FAIL** or **REWRITE** — and where it is FAIL or REWRITE, say what is wrong in the words a person would use, and give the replacement wording in full (never as a tail beginning with "…").
