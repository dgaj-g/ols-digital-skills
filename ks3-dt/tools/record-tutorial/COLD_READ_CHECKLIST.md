# THE COLD-READ CHECKLIST — v2, 27 August 2026

**This file is what the judge is handed, and nothing else.** It is committed and versioned
so that what the judge was told is always inspectable: if a sentence reaches a pupil badly
written, the question "what was the reader actually asked?" has an answer in the repository
rather than in somebody's memory.

The judge receives exactly two things: **the rendered pupil transcript** (the screens in the
order she meets them, with no spec, no diff, no design notes and no authoring conversation)
and **this file**. Nothing else. That is DFM 270, and it is a rule about the reader's
context, not about their diligence.

---

## 0. THE DEFAULT IS FAIL

**A sentence FAILS unless it reads clean aloud at the reader's age. "Not wrong" is not a
pass.** Grammatical is not a defence. A sentence can be perfectly grammatical, carry no
banned word, break no rule in any file, and still be sloppy writing that a twelve-year-old
stumbles over — and that is precisely the class this checklist exists to catch, because no
mechanical net can.

Where you are unsure, the answer is FAIL. A sentence you had to read twice has already
failed. A sentence you had to defend has already failed (DFM 231: *when a wording has to be
defended, that is the answer, not the start of a negotiation*).

**The reader is an eleven or twelve-year-old, in every year group** (DFM 256). Not a
thirteen-year-old for J3. Voice changes between years; reading age does not.

---

## 1. THE READER, AND WHERE SHE IS SITTING

She is eleven or twelve. She is at a school C2k Windows machine with a **mouse** (never
"tap") and a **wired connection** (never "the wifi"). Computers are side by side. Google
Drive and OneDrive are both available. Classmates share first names. **There may be no
teacher in the room** — a cover day, or catching up at home — so on that screen, the screen
is the teacher (DFM 53).

What is scarce for her is **reading age and confidence**. Spend words freely; keep each
sentence short. One idea per sentence, as many sentences as understanding needs.

---

## 2. THE THREE QUESTIONS, ON EVERY SENTENCE (DFM 24/138)

Read it **aloud**, cold, from her seat. Then answer:

1. **Can she DO it** where she is sitting?
2. **Can she PICTURE every noun** in it — has she met that thing, on a screen she has
   already seen, in this transcript?
3. **Can she SAY what it is for**?

Any "no" is a FAIL. And one more, from DFM 193(d): **what would she ASK here?** Any question
she would have to ask is a fail, because there may be nobody to ask.

## 3. THE WORKED MUST-FAIL EXAMPLES — HIS OWN, FROM 27 AUGUST 2026

These are calibration. Every one of them passed a judged read and then failed HIS read. If
your judgement would have passed any of these, it is set too generously.

| MUST FAIL | why it fails |
|---|---|
| *"…and then uses what they typed in what it says next."* | A preposition pile-up. "typed in in what…" is what the mouth does with it. Grammatical, and unreadable aloud. |
| *"That is the whole hour."* | A clipped declarative on a bare demonstrative. "That" points at nothing inside the sentence. |
| *"Never one instead of the other."* | A negation-led fragment with no verb and no subject. |
| *"Two questions means two boxes, and they need different names. Two boxes called the same thing is one box…"* | Telegraphic, and it uses the metaphor ("box") with the real word (variable) nowhere on the surface. |
| *"Exact matters here."* | Two words doing the work of a sentence. |
| *"Four fixes, one game."* | A chain of verbless clauses on a comma. |
| *"no points, nothing needs it, here for spare minutes now"* | Three fragments on commas. |
| *"Your job is the room, the two stops, and the questions that get a stuck pupil looking at her own console."* | (Teacher register.) Compressed to the point of costing meaning, and "the two stops" is used before it is defined. |
| *"Found the studio"* | Reads as past-tense "find". A word that means two things is a word she has to choose between. |
| *"before the rally"* / *"thirty seconds of protection"* | An event name used as shared context the card never gives; an abstract metaphor where a concrete action belongs. |

## 3b. THE MUST-PASS EXEMPLARS — his own approved tone

If your judgement would fail these, it is set too harshly.

- *"Build each rung (each activity) in the SAME project that you created for the video
  tutorial you've just completed on the previous screen, which you called make-it-move. The
  project name stays the same as you climb the ladder and complete each new activity — you
  never start a new one."* (his own wording, DFM 188a)
- *"You'll see the yellow light flashing on the micro:bit; when it stops, it's loaded!"*
- The Lesson 1 welcome card, the "One last thing, before you go" belonging card, and the
  Vault chat (DFM 83).
- *"a box with a name on it (a variable)"* — the metaphor and the real name travelling
  together (DFM 267g/268).

## 4. THE SHAPE RULES — mechanical enough to be sure about

- **No fragment chains.** A verbless clause joined to another by a comma is banned in pupil
  prose (DFM 192b). One idea per sentence, and each sentence has a verb.
- **Sequences are numbered lists**, one number per line (DFM 135/171), never prose.
- **The action comes first** on a task card: what she is asked to DO, then how, then what the
  finished thing should be (DFM 151). A description of the trap before the instruction is
  the wrong order.
- **Define before use, on EVERY surface** (DFM 13/138.1.3). A term defined three screens ago
  on a different card is not defined here if she can arrive here first.
- **The person leads the sentence.** "You…", never "The website will…".
- **Concrete nouns.** "the micro:bit", "the black box you type in" — never "the device", "the
  system", "the build".
- **True to the screen and true to the room** (DFM 35). If it says the demo is here, the demo
  is here. If it says three, there are three.
- **A box is always paired with the real word** at its first use on a surface: "a box (a
  variable)" or "a box with a name on it — a variable" (DFM 268).
- **UK English.** No "tap", no "the wifi", no US spellings.
- **No cleverness**: no idioms, no poetry, no personification, no design rationale on stage,
  no invented staff names, no fake precision.

---

## 5. THE PER-ITEM BLOCK — every marked, baseline or recap question (DFM 233/235)

Answer all five, per item, in writing. A tick is not a judgement.

1. **Exactly one defensible answer?** If a careful reader at her age can argue a second
   option — including one that merely DESCRIBES the key's outcome in other words — it is
   broken. A true-but-partial key fails the same way.
2. **Is the scenario anchored?** A real actor in a place she can picture. Never "a club", "a
   program", "a system".
3. **Does the difficulty measure anything?** An item everyone gets right measures nothing.
4. **Do the options hand the answer over?** No joke distractors, no odd-one-out by length,
   no stem containing its own answer.
5. **Can you say the item's point in one sentence?** If not, it goes.

Plus: **every pronoun in a scenario points BACKWARD at a name already given** (DFM 267d).

## 6. HOW TO FILE IT (DFM 270 + J13a4 — it is written for HIM to read)

The verdict file opens by naming the transcript artefact and its **TRANSCRIPT HASH**, copied
from the transcript's own header. The pack refuses a lesson under review whose transcript
hash does not match the one its verdict file names — so a verdict cannot be spent on text
that has since changed.

Then: **one row per sentence judged**, in plain English, **quoting the sentence**, so he can
open the file and read what the judge said about any line before he ever sits the lesson.
No trade jargon, no rule numbers standing in for a reason.

**Every READ-FIRST CANDIDATE named by the language gate must have its own row, answered
individually.** The gate emits that list by name; the pack checks each one is answered. This
is the machine's guarantee that a risky sentence cannot ride a general pass — the judge reads
everything, and the named ones cannot be skimmed.

A row reads:

```
| VERDICT | where it is | the sentence, quoted | what a reader would trip over, or why it is clean |
```

**PASS**, **FAIL** or **REWRITE** — and where it is FAIL or REWRITE, say what is wrong in the
words a person would use, not in rule numbers.
